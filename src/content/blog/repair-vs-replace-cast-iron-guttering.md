"Can this be repaired?" is one of the most common questions asked about cast-iron guttering on older properties, and the honest answer depends on where exactly the failure is. Here's what a proper repair actually involves, step by step.

## Step 1: Assess the extent of corrosion

Cast iron corrodes from the inside, so surface rust visible from below isn't a reliable guide to how sound a section actually is. A proper assessment involves checking thickness at multiple points along a run, not just looking at the worst-looking section - a gutter can look uniformly rusty while only being genuinely compromised in one or two spots.

## Step 2: Decide section by section, not run by run

Repair doesn't have to mean all-or-nothing. A typical repair job replaces individual perforated or badly corroded sections with matching cast-iron profile, while re-sealing and repainting sections that are structurally sound but cosmetically tired. This is usually far cheaper than a wholesale replacement and preserves more of the original material - see our [cast iron vs uPVC guide](/blog/cast-iron-vs-upvc-guttering-terraced-housing/) for when each makes more sense.

## Step 3: Reseal joints properly

Traditional cast-iron guttering uses overlapping joints sealed with a mastic or putty compound, which degrades over time and is a common source of leaks even where the iron itself is sound. Resealing joints properly - cleaning back to bare metal, applying a modern flexible sealant rather than old-style putty - often fixes a leak that looks like it needs full replacement.

## Step 4: Treat and prime before repainting

Any exposed rust needs to be mechanically removed (wire brush or grinding) and treated with a rust-inhibiting primer before repainting - painting straight over active rust just traps moisture underneath and the corrosion continues beneath the new paint.

## Step 5: Check and refit brackets

Cast-iron guttering is heavy, and brackets that have loosened or corroded put ongoing strain on joints even after a repair. Refitting or replacing brackets as part of the same job, rather than treating them as separate, is worth insisting on.

## When repair genuinely isn't the answer

If corrosion has gone through the metal in multiple, widely spread locations along a run, patching individual sections stops being cost-effective compared with a full replacement of that run - still potentially in matching cast iron if the property requires it, rather than a wholesale switch to uPVC.
