Most homeowners never think about whether their guttering is actually sized correctly - it's just "the gutter that came with the house." But undersized guttering and downpipes are a real, common cause of recurring overflow that no amount of cleaning will fix, and England's Building Regulations set out exactly how sizing should be calculated.

## The governing standard

Approved Document H (the Building Regulations guidance covering drainage and waste disposal) uses a design rainfall intensity of 75mm/hour for domestic roof drainage in England, and references BS EN 12056-3 as the detailed calculation method for gutter and rainwater pipe sizing based on roof area and expected rainfall.

## What actually determines correct sizing

- **Roof area draining to each gutter run** - a larger roof area needs proportionally more gutter and downpipe capacity
- **Gutter profile and fall** - a gutter laid to a proper fall, a deeper section, or a round-edged outlet can allow a smaller overall size for the same capacity
- **Number and spacing of downpipes** - a single downpipe serving a long gutter run can be a bottleneck even if the gutter itself is adequately sized, and a rainwater pipe should be at least the size of the gutter outlet it serves

## Common real-world sizing problems

- An extension added later that drains into existing guttering never sized for the extra roof area
- A single downpipe on an older property that was adequate when installed but now runs to capacity more often
- Standard "off the shelf" gutter profiles used on a large roof without checking whether a larger profile is actually needed

If a property has genuinely persistent overflow that isn't explained by blockage, undersizing is worth ruling in or out before assuming the guttering just needs replacing like-for-like.
